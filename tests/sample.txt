Step 1 — Internal-first: Persuadable Research consumer findings; EnteriCare technology; internal brand/equity documents
- The 2013 study titled “Claims Sort Study - Fish Oil Supplement” (Persuadable Research Corporation) identified which claims are most motivating to buy, why those claims are persuasive, and the credibility of each claim. Method: 400 online interviews (Oct 15–18, 2013) with adults 25–64 who use OTC fish oil, primary household shoppers, balanced gender, and a specified mix of frequent shoppers at Sam’s Club, Walgreens, CVS, and Walmart. Respondents evaluated 27 claims across groups: Fishy Burps reduction, Absorption benefits, Product Features, Additional Benefits, Benefits vs. Mega Red, and Mini Gel Features (Mini Gel only for Purchase Motivation). They rated Purchase Motivation, Believability, and Uniqueness, and ranked claims within each group; rankings mirrored ratings. Q.12 brand purchase data showed distribution across 10 brands; one brand at 38% share, others up to 16% or lower. [1]
- The new fish oil softgel concept tested was designed to maximize absorption, increase Omega-3 levels, and control fishy burps using enteric coating; it was claimed to be the first and only clinically proven to be absorbed and to deliver 3x Omega-3 versus MegaRed krill oil at a fraction of the cost. The softgels were described as clear, 100% all-natural. Recommendations emphasized promoting the heart-health benefit supported by Omega-3 content, and suggested price-differential messaging with MegaRed; exact price differentials should be tested at multiple price points. Survey results showed “Nothing” as the most popular response due to high satisfaction, with a lack of specific mentions of fishy burps suggesting taste/smell responses may underlie some reactions. Purchase intent data for a store-brand fish oil softgel is referenced. © 2013 Persuadable Research Corporation. [1]
- Purchase intent for the store-brand fish oil softgel: around 30% definitely buy; top-two-box scores range 71%–78%. “Clinically proven to increase Omega-3 levels” motivated at total 81%, Nature Made 78%, Mega Red purchasers 91%. “Clinically proven to increase Omega-3 levels” had the greatest impact on purchase intent for the Total sample. “Provides 3X the Omega-3 levels vs MegaRed krill oil”: total 79%, Nature Made 69%, Mega Red purchasers 93%. “Clinically proven absorption”: total 71%, Nature Made 70%, Mega Red purchasers 76%. [1]
- Consumers were highly satisfied with their current brand. “Increasing Omega-3 levels has the greatest impact on purchase intent for Total Sample and the key brands shown below.” Brand-specific satisfaction drivers:
  - Nature Made: “the smell is pretty bad, and I am not sure if it gives me all the benefits it would”
  - Mega Red: “least amount of aftertaste; cholesterol has come down; no fishy taste; smaller pill; easy to swallow; supply lasts 30 days.”
  - Nature’s Bounty: “a little smaller pill would be nice; less odor, less after taste; price and availability are an additional plus to quality of ingredients and amounts per pill; it provides the dosage I want at an affordable price; doesn’t have a fishy smell or taste; helps my nervous system and metabolism.” [1]
- Heart health claims were among the top motivators and the most believable, but not unique. Benefits evaluated included: maintain heart health; maintain cardiovascular health; may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease; maintain healthy triglyceride levels; brain health; eye health; blood pressure in the normal range. Believability across benefits reached up to 94%/91% for certain groups; motivation to buy (Q.54): Total = 44%, Nature Made = 41%, Nature’s Bounty = 47%, Mega Red = 44%, All Others = 47%. [1]
- Reasons for the most motivating claim: The most motivating purchase benefit identified was “Helps maintain heart health,” with open-ended responses such as “A healthy heart is important to me” and “my heart is my primary concern.” Many consumers viewed the heart as a “weakest link,” prioritizing strengthening and protection. The study compared brand performance across Motivation To Buy, Believability, and Uniqueness for claims like reducing CHD risk and managing triglycerides. A follow-up (Q.65) further assessed how much each benefit would motivate purchase for a new fish oil softgel product. [1]
- Eye health provided the greatest motivation to buy among the listed benefits in one evaluation set; benefits evaluated included heart health, cardiovascular health, brain health, eye health, blood pressure in normal range, and CHD risk reduction. Results presented as “Definitely Motivate To Buy” and “Probably Motivate To Buy” across Total and brand-level groups. [1]
- Sample profile and claims tested list: Base N=400 with demographic breakdowns (e.g., White 79%; Married 63%; income brackets incl. 8% $85k–$99,999; 7% $100k–$124,999; etc.). Claims tested emphasized new technology and all-natural clear softgel to control fishy burps; 3x more Omega-3 than MegaRed; clinically proven to control fishy burps; once-a-day dosage; clear softgel; 40% smaller mini gel; clinically proven absorption; helps maintain heart health. [1]
- A concept statement described an all-natural, clear softgel fish oil supplement designed to prevent fishy burps while delivering Omega-3. It is clinically proven to control fishy burps and to markedly enhance Omega-3 absorption, with a once-daily dosage. Each 1000 mg fish oil softgel provides the same Omega-3 strength as standard formulations, but is offered in ultra-concentrated mini gels that are 40% smaller for easier swallowing. Step 7 — Regulatory constraints and opportunities for Omega-3 claims (FDA/FTC U.S.)
- FDA qualified health claims (QHCs) for EPA/DHA:
  - June 19, 2019 FDA announcement: no intent to object to certain qualified health claims stating that consuming EPA and DHA omega-3 fatty acids in foods or dietary supplements may reduce the risk of hypertension and coronary heart disease. Evidence was “credible” but “inconsistent and inconclusive,” not meeting “significant scientific agreement.” One enforcement discretion factor: products must contain at least 0.8 g combined EPA+DHA per serving. Claims must include qualifying language describing evidence strength to avoid deception. [21][22]
  - 2000 FDA letter (Pearson v. Shalala context): EPA/DHA omega-3s are safe and lawful up to 3 g/day from all sources; lack of significant scientific agreement for a CHD risk-reduction claim in the general population, but scientific evidence in support of a qualified claim outweighs evidence against it; newer interventions show CHD risk reduction in diseased populations; observational results mixed; uncertainty remains for general population. [24]
- FTC enforcement and guidance:
  - 2010 FTC warning letters to 11 marketers of children’s omega-3 supplements: claims about boosting brain/vision development in kids may violate FTC Act if not supported by scientific evidence; two weeks to respond; example: L’il Critters modified marketing materials to ensure compliance. [21]
  - 2010 FTC settlement with NBTY (NatureSmart/Rexall Sundown): deceptive health claims in Disney/Marvel-branded children’s multivitamins; misrepresented amount of DHA and unsubstantiated brain/eye development claims; barred from misrepresenting ingredient amounts/benefits; $2.1M refunds. [22]
  - 2021 FTC settlement with BASF SE and DIEM Labs: deceptively marketing Hepaxa/Hepaxa PD as clinically proven to reduce liver fat in NAFLD; required competent and reliable scientific evidence; barred from misrepresentation; more than $416,000 for refunds; prohibits claims of reducing liver fat/cure diseases without randomized human clinical testing. Final consent orders approved 4-0. [23][24]
  - Broader FTC notices to hundreds of companies about health claims (context for supplements advertising compliance). [25]
- Additional clinical/medical POVs influencing claims sensitivity:
  - Cleveland Clinic (2023) commentary: OTC fish oil supplements provide little to no cardiovascular benefit and may pose risks; large studies have not consistently shown CVD risk reductions with OTC doses; prescription fish oil data mixed with concerns about placebo effects and potential increased atrial fibrillation risk; suggests dietary fish instead and clinician consultation. [15]
- Safety/intake guidance:
  - FDA maximum daily intake: 3 g/day combined DHA+EPA; no more than 2 g from supplements (Everyday Health summary). [15]
